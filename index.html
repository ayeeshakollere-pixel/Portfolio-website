Canonical: Reading a Private Company’s Momentum

How do you judge a company that publishes no quarterly earnings?

What I did

Canonical doesn’t file quarterly earnings, so I rebuilt its story from three public signals that most people miss.

The three signals

Developers pick Ubuntu. 27.8% of software developers use it, the most popular Linux choice. When developers love a tool, companies eventually pay to support it.

It’s everywhere in the cloud. More than 60% of public cloud servers run Ubuntu, with about 67,000 new instances launched every day.

Companies pay for it. Canonical earned $292M in 2024 with an 83% gross margin. Once the product is built, selling it costs very little.

Revenue grew from $205M in 2022 to $292M in 2024, and it did so profitably. Headcount rose 37% to support more customers. That is a healthy way to scale.

Airline Fuel Shock: Who Protected Their Profits?

Delta, United, Ryanair and easyJet, compared after the 2026 jump in jet fuel prices.

The question I asked

When fuel prices doubled, which airlines protected their profits, and what did they do differently?

I compared four airlines using their own company reports and explained the results in plain language.

What I found

Ryanair and easyJet had locked in fuel prices early, so their fuel bills rose only 16–17% in April to June. Delta’s and United’s rose 77–84%.

Paying less for fuel didn’t mean losing less profit. All four airlines kept less profit from every $100 of sales. Delta and United earned about 12% more per seat, while Ryanair’s average fare fell 6% and easyJet earned 3% less per seat.

Why it matters

Locked-in prices run out. Ryanair has locked in only 25% of its fuel for the next year, at a higher price of about $95 a barrel.
